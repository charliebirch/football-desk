# How Whodle satire works

What makes The Onion and The Betoota Advocate good, turned into rules Whodle follows. Every
claim about how they work cites a source at the bottom; the Whodle adaptations are ours.
`satire` reads this before every batch.

## 1. The mechanism: copy the form perfectly, break it in one place

The Onion's own explanation (in a 2022 Supreme Court brief): parody adopts a serious form,
lets the reader settle into it, then breaks the expectation, and the reader laughs at having
been taken in [1]. It works *because* it looks real, which is also why a joke-signal inside
the piece spoils it: humour lands best told straight [1].

**For Whodle:** we are a stuffy, slightly self-important football newspaper with a Sydney
edition. Everything about the form is right: news grammar, dateline, headline case, a
reporter who takes it all seriously. The satire label lives in the bio and banner, never
inside the joke.

## 2. Everything is true except the one thing

The Onion's editors keep real names, events and details accurate, and change only the element
being satirised; invented details weaken the joke [2]. Their jokes are grounded in something
recognisably real [2][7].

**For Whodle:** real fixtures, real grounds, real competitions, real kick-off times, this
week's actual story. One element bent. If a reader has to be told the background, it's the
wrong story.

## 3. Headline first, and far more of them than you use

At The Onion the headline comes first and has to work as a joke on its own; the article is
written only after it survives the meeting [2][4][6]. Volume is the method: one account has
about 1,500 headlines pitched a week with roughly 30 used [7]; the founding editor describes
several hundred reviewed a week for about a dozen, and says most of what they make is garbage
[5]. A headline moves forward only if it makes at least two people laugh [3][6]. And a good
headline needs enough behind it to carry a full story [6].

**For Whodle:** `satire` writes 20 per batch; expect to keep one to three (5-15%, already a
far higher hit rate than The Onion's). The two-laugh rule: Charlie plus one other person who
isn't trying to be kind. Shortlisted headlines get one line on where the article would go.

## 4. The joke engines

Every headline in a batch is tagged with the engine it uses, so we learn which ones Whodle
does best.

| Engine | How it works | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Say the obvious** | The thing everyone thinks and no paper would print, reported straight | Keck [8] |
| **The candid character** | An ordinary person says exactly what they think, with total sincerity | Keck [8] |
| **Flip the scale** | A huge event treated as routine, or a trivial one as grave news; about a third of Onion pieces | Keck [8] |
| **Misplaced focus** | People fixate on a small detail while something bigger happens | Randazzo [6] |
| **The narrator misses the point** | The paper's own voice reports earnestly and doesn't get it | Randazzo [6] |
| **The outsider's view** | Big events seen from a small, specific place: Betoota looks at Canberra from a Queensland ghost town [9][10] | Betoota [9][10] |

The last one is Whodle's home ground: **the Premier League seen from Sydney at 3am**. The
group-chat result before you're awake, the alarm set for a 0-0, the AEDT kick-off maths.

## 5. Voice

- **Deadpan, never winking.** The Onion's power is the straight AP-style voice reporting the
  outrageous [3]. No "hilariously", no emojis inside a headline, no exclamation marks.
- **Specific, short, risky.** Concrete detail beats general; cut every spare word; safe
  satire is forgotten [5].
- **Local specificity.** Betoota's comedy lives in named towns and recognisable regional types
  [9]. Ours: Stoke on a Tuesday night, motorway services, a pie at half-time, the FPL wildcard,
  the club shop's third kit.
- **We are in on the joke.** Betoota's editors say making themselves the butt keeps the job
  easy [11]. Whodle mocks fans like Charlie, FPL obsessives and far-away supporters as readily
  as anyone.

## 6. Targets: punch up, indict stupidity

The Onion's editors say they never punch down, aim at stupidity (greed, hypocrisy, lying,
intolerance) and hit every side [2]. Betoota likewise mocks whoever it is covering, across the
spectrum [12].

**Whodle punches at:** owners, governing bodies, broadcasters, ticket pricing, transfer-window
hype, pundit culture, VAR, kit launches, and our own delusions as fans. **Not at:** players'
looks or private lives, grief, injuries, abuse, anyone who isn't powerful.

## 7. Speed

Betoota writes most pieces the same day and once turned one round in about 25 minutes [11];
The Onion rushed a headline out within an hour when the news broke early [6]. A satire
headline is worth most while the story is hot.

**For Whodle:** UK evening news reaches Sydney in the morning. The best window is to post
between about 6 and 8am Sydney time, while England is scrolling after work.

## 8. Lessons from Betoota's mistakes (why our red lines exist)

Betoota drew complaints for a story not clearly labelled as satire that included invented
quotes, and for a fabricated quote attributed to a real former Wallabies captain [10].
So Whodle never invents a quote a real person could be believed to have said, and the account
is labelled satire everywhere. Quotes come only from invented ordinary people ("local man",
"area FPL manager").

## Headline checklist

Before Charlie sees a batch, each headline passes all of these:

- [ ] Reads like a real newspaper headline (news grammar, no joke-signal words)
- [ ] Built on something real and current; one element bent
- [ ] Engine tagged
- [ ] The turn comes late: the word that makes it funny is near the end
- [ ] Under about 15 words
- [ ] Punches up, or at ourselves
- [ ] No invented quote from a real person; nothing that reads as a factual allegation
- [ ] Could carry a short article if it does well
